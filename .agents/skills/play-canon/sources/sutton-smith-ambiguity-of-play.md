## Core concepts

- **Ambiguity.** Play is and is not what it appears to be: the playful nip connotes a
  bite, but not what a bite connotes (p. 1). Play involves all seven of Empson's types
  of ambiguity: of reference, referent, intent, sense, transition, contradiction, and
  meaning ("is it play or playfighting?") (p. 2). The book asks that play's definition
  be broad: passive and vicarious forms as well as active ones, adults as well as
  children, and not limited to the modern Western values of nonproductive, rational,
  voluntary, and fun. Play always has its own performances and stylizations, and like
  language, it isn't good or bad in itself (pp. 218-219).
- **Rhetorics.** A rhetoric is a persuasive discourse or implicit narrative, adopted
  knowingly or not by members of an affiliation to persuade others that their beliefs
  are true and worthwhile (p. 8). Each has a history, a function, a form of play, its
  own players, and its own disciplines (p. 215). Four are ancient (fate, power, identity,
  frivolity), and three are modern (progress, the imaginary, the self) (pp. 10-11). The
  ancient ones are about groups, are often obligatory rather than optional, and imply
  that play can be coercive. The modern ones say play is an exercise of freedom
  (pp. 52-53). The seven:
  - **Progress:** animals and children, but not adults, adapt and develop through play.
    The belief is more often assumed than shown; educators came to see play as being
    about development rather than enjoyment (pp. 9-10).
  - **Fate:** play and life are ruled by destiny, gods, or luck, and very little by
    ourselves, except through magic. Usually applied to gambling and games of chance,
    and probably the oldest rhetoric (p. 10). It is the most pervasive rhetoric, and today the
    least openly advocated (p. 53).
  - **Power:** usually applied to sports and contests. Play represents conflict and
    fortifies the status of those who control it or are its heroes (p. 10).
  - **Identity:** usually applied to community celebrations and festivals. The play
    tradition confirms, maintains, or advances the power and identity of the community
    of players (p. 10).
  - **The imaginary:** usually applied to improvisation, in literature and elsewhere. It
    idealizes imagination, flexibility, and creativity, though play's flexibility also
    includes large amounts of inversion and irrationality (p. 11).
  - **The self:** usually applied to hobbies or high-risk play such as bungee jumping.
    Play is idealized through the player's experience (fun, relaxation, escape) and its
    intrinsic or aesthetic satisfactions (p. 11); its function is peak experience
    (p. 215).
  - **Frivolity:** the work-ethic view that play is idle or foolish, and also the
    trickster and the fool, who enacted playful protest against the ordained order
    (p. 11). Its essences are nonsense and inversion (p. 201).
- **Rhetoric and practice.** No rhetoric (the argument made about a play form) is the
  same as the practice (the way the form is played), but because the rhetoric states
  the form's value, the two are easily conflated (p. 214). "When the adult says play is a
  developmental experience, for the child it may be nothing but hide-and-seek" (p. 216).
- **Interests.** The group that maintains a rhetoric benefits by hegemony over the
  players, their competitors, or those excluded from the play (p. 16). Each rhetoric has
  its own disciplines: progress in biology, psychology, and education; fate in
  mathematics; power in sociology and history; identity in anthropology and folklore;
  the imaginary in art and literature; the self in psychiatry; frivolity in pop culture
  (p. 215). Players' own definitions of their play often bear very little relation to
  the theorists' (p. 16).
- **Variability.** What all play forms share is their diversity and variability
  (p. 221). Structurally, play is quirky, redundant, and flexible (p. 229): unpredictable
  and nonsensical, endlessly reproduced (house, trucks, ball games), and dependent on
  flexibility to learn and perform (pp. 222-224). Play engenders "variable
  contingencies" (uncertainties and risks) so that players can exercise control over
  them in fictive or factual terms (p. 229). The book's definition: psychologically,
  play is a virtual simulation of staged contingencies, with control won through
  mastery or through "further chaos"; players stylize existential themes that mimic or
  mock the uncertainties and risks of survival (p. 231).
- **Persistence.** Play keeps alive through life the young child's "unrealistic
  optimism, egocentricity, and reactivity", which the book calls "guarantors of
  persistence in the face of adversity" (p. 231). Older players show it too (p. 227).

## Guidance

### Interactions

- Let the same activity carry more than one reading: a contest for one player, a story
  for another. (Ambiguity)
- Leave room for inversion and nonsense: silly moves, tolerated rule-breaking, role
  reversals. (Frivolity)
- For identity play, give the group its own rituals, festival moments, and traditions.
  (Identity)
- For power play, decide who gains status from the play (the winners, the organizers,
  or the house) and make that visible. (Power, Interests)

### Structures

- Name the rhetoric the work is for, then make the structure deliver it. Progress needs
  visible growth, power needs a contest, identity needs a group and its festival, the
  imaginary needs room to improvise, the self needs a peak experience, fate needs
  chance, and frivolity needs license for nonsense. (Rhetorics)
- Build in variability: more than one way to play, and room for uses nobody planned.
  (Variability)
- Build redundancy: a form players can replay endlessly with small changes, the way
  house or a ball game is replayed. (Variability)
- Stage uncertainty the player can bring under control through mastery, or tip into
  gleeful chaos. Both are play. (Variability)
- Check the claim against the players' own account of what they are doing, not only
  the maker's. (Interests)

### Content

- Deliver the claimed purpose through the play itself. An educational game teaches
  through its play, not in the gaps between turns. (Rhetoric and practice)
- Let play mimic or mock real risks (danger, loss, rivalry) on a stage where they can
  be survived. (Variability)

### Style

- Let play look like play. Don't dress fun up as work to justify it, or dress work up as
  fun to sell it. (Interests)
- Keep failure cheap and retries quick, so optimism and persistence survive a loss.
  (Persistence)

## Anti-patterns

- **Rhetoric laundering.** The work is justified with one rhetoric (usually progress:
  "it's educational") while the actual experience delivers another, such as a power
  contest or a fate machine. (Rhetoric and practice, Interests)
- **Single-purpose flattening.** The work is optimized for one outcome until the
  ambiguity and the nonsense are gone. (Ambiguity, Frivolity)
- **Adult agendas overwriting children's play.** Progress goals displace the play
  children actually choose. (Progress, Interests)
- **Frivolity treated as waste.** Silliness is cut because it can't be justified. The
  work-ethic view of play as a waste of time has been the strongest rhetoric of the
  last four hundred years (p. 201). (Frivolity)
- **One lens only.** The work is judged through one rhetoric, usually progress, so the
  play that rhetoric can't see goes unnoticed and unbuilt. (Interests)

## Review criterion

- **Set:** Sutton-Smith
- **Source:** *The Ambiguity of Play* (1997), Sutton-Smith
- **Concepts:** Ambiguity, Rhetorics (Progress, Fate, Power, Identity, The imaginary,
  The self, Frivolity), Rhetoric and practice, Interests, Variability, Persistence
- **Criterion:** The rhetoric the work claims is the one it delivers, the interests it
  serves include the player's, and there is room for play beyond a single purpose.
- **Question seeds:**
  - Which rhetoric does the piece claim, which does the artifact deliver, and do they
    match?
  - Whose interests does that rhetoric serve: the player's, or the maker's or buyer's?
  - Where is the room for nonsense, inversion, or play the maker didn't plan?
- **Misapplied when:**
  - a rhetoric is treated as wrong, or a single rhetoric is forced onto play that mixes
    several. The book holds that people can't live without these value systems and
    doesn't treat them as negative (p. 9), and each rhetoric has many variants (p. 11).
    The failure is a mismatch or a concealment.
  - play is failed only for being obligatory, vicarious, or unproductive. The book
    counts those limits as modern Western values, not universals (pp. 218-219).
  - Variability is scored as settled theory. The book calls its own proposal a
    "metaphorical melange" (p. 218) and admits it is one more rhetoric of progress
    (p. 231).
- **Default binding:** yes

## Calibration plant

A "learning game" whose only play is a leaderboard race through quiz questions, claimed
as progress. The critic should cite **Rhetoric and practice** or **Rhetorics** (progress
claimed, power delivered).

## Fit

- **Strong for:** anything sold with a purpose: educational, therapeutic, wellness,
  marketing, or team-building play.
- **Weak for:** pure mechanics questions that the claim doesn't frame.
- **Tension:** Huizinga (Free), Caillois (Six qualities), and Carse (Obligation) make
  voluntariness part of what play is; this book counts it as a modern Western value
  (pp. 52-53, 218-219). The
  book's table also files Huizinga under power, Csikszentmihalyi under the self, and
  Bateson under the imaginary (p. 215), and reads Huizinga's play-as-contest as the
  power rhetoric (p. 80). When this set is chosen with those, send findings that rest on
  "play must be free" to the owner as a tension.
