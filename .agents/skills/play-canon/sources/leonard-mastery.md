## Core concepts

- **The mastery curve.** Mastery is a process, a journey, not a goal or destination
  (p. 5). Learning any new skill involves brief spurts of progress, each followed by a
  slight decline to a plateau somewhat higher than the one before (p. 14).
- **The plateau.** A life of mastery is spent mostly on the plateau (p. 40). The master
  practices primarily for the sake of the practice itself, and learns to enjoy the
  plateau as much as the upward surges (p. 17). We are taught to value the product, the
  prize, and the climactic moment instead (p. 39). "To love the plateau is to love what
  is most essential and enduring in your life" (p. 49).
- **The Dabbler.** Loves the rituals of getting started, the equipment, the lingo, and
  "the shine of newness". The first plateau is unacceptable, so it's on to something new
  (pp. 19-20).
- **The Obsessive.** Wants results fast. At a plateau, redoubles the effort, takes
  shortcuts, and rides spurts and sharp declines toward a sure fall (pp. 21-23).
- **The Hacker.** After getting the hang of a thing, is willing to stay on the plateau
  indefinitely, skipping stages that mastery needs (p. 23). The types mix: one can be a
  Dabbler in one field and a master in another, or move between types in the same field
  (p. 24).
- **The five keys.** Five keys keep a person on the path:
  - instruction: for most skills, first-rate instruction beats teaching yourself (p. 55);
  - practice: best understood as a noun, a path traveled for its own sake (pp. 73-74);
  - surrender: to the teacher and the discipline, and of hard-won proficiency, with a
    willingness to feel clumsy (p. 81);
  - intentionality: the mental game, such as picturing a move before making it (p. 89);
  - the edge: masters are zealots of the fundamentals who also push past previous
    limits (p. 97). Playing the edge is a balancing act that needs awareness of safe
    limits (p. 99).
- **Homeostasis.** Everyone resists significant change, whether for the worse or for
  the better (p. 107). Self-regulating systems, from a body to a family to a culture,
  hold themselves steady and snap back when changed (pp. 107-108). The book's
  guidelines: expect resistance and backlash (p. 114), negotiate with your resistance
  (p. 115), build a support system (p. 116), follow a regular practice, and commit to
  lifelong learning (p. 117).
- **Pitfalls.** The book lists pitfalls along the path (from p. 133), including:
  - obsessive goal orientation: wanting quick, sure, visible results (p. 134);
  - prizes and medals: external rewards that slow or stop the journey. Gold stars speed
    children's learning at first, then progress levels off, and when the stars stop it
    falls below that of children who never got them (p. 137);
  - vanity: learning anything significant means being willing to look foolish (p. 138);
  - dead seriousness: without humor the path narrows to tunnel vision (p. 139);
  - inconsistency: regular practice, at a regular time and place, is the mark of the
    master (p. 139);
  - perfectionism: comparing yourself with polished expert performances, or setting
    standards no one could meet. Mastery is a process, not perfection (p. 140).

- **The fool.** "To be a learner, you've got to be willing to be a fool" (p. 172): the
  fool of the medieval jester and of the tarot card numbered zero, the emptiness that
  lets something new come in (pp. 172-173). An infant learns to talk because a babbled
  "da" is celebrated as a step toward "daddy"; if every attempt were corrected, the
  child might never learn to talk (p. 174).

## Guidance

### Interactions

- Make the basic move satisfying to repeat, so practice itself is rewarding. (The
  plateau)
- Provide instruction: show the moves through a demo, a guide, or a teacher. (The five
  keys: instruction)
- Offer optional challenges past the comfortable level, taken by choice and with safe
  limits in view. (The five keys: the edge)
- Make it safe to look clumsy: early attempts can be funny without being humiliating.
  (The fool, Pitfalls: vanity)
- Reward approximations: greet an early attempt as a step toward the move, not as a
  wrong answer. (The fool)

### Structures

- Design plateaus deliberately: long stretches where the practice is rewarding,
  punctuated by small, visible spurts of progress, with the dip before each plateau
  expected rather than punished. (The mastery curve)
- Put depth under the surface: fundamentals that keep yielding more the longer you
  practice them. (The plateau)
- Build onboarding that expects resistance: ease people in, give them a support system,
  and plan for backsliding. (Homeostasis)
- Support a regular rhythm of practice, such as a set time, a set place, or a small
  starting ritual. (Pitfalls: inconsistency)

### Content

- Teach fundamentals first, and write content that rewards coming back to the basics.
  (The five keys: practice)

### Style

- Use a patient tone that celebrates practice, not only milestones, and keep humor in
  it. (The plateau, Pitfalls: dead seriousness)

## Anti-patterns

- **The novelty treadmill.** A steady stream of new content that feeds the Dabbler and
  hides a lack of depth. (The Dabbler)
- **Escalating rewards.** Bigger and faster payouts that feed the Obsessive until the
  crash. (The Obsessive)
- **Gold-star decay.** External rewards that speed early progress, then level off, and
  leave players worse off when they stop. (Pitfalls: prizes and medals)
- **No invitation past comfort.** Nothing asks the player to leave the familiar plateau.
  (The Hacker)
- **Depth with no instruction.** Mastery is possible, but nothing shows how to learn the
  moves. (The five keys: instruction)
- **Correcting the babble.** Early attempts met with correction instead of
  encouragement, so players stop trying. (The fool)
- **Perfection on display.** Only flawless expert performances are shown, so a
  beginner's attempts look worthless. (Pitfalls: perfectionism)

## Review criterion

- **Set:** Leonard
- **Source:** *Mastery: The Keys to Success and Long-Term Fulfillment* (1991), Leonard
- **Concepts:** The mastery curve, The plateau, The Dabbler, The Obsessive, The Hacker,
  The five keys, Homeostasis, Pitfalls, The fool
- **Criterion:** Past the first plateau there is a practice worth returning to. The work
  rewards practice itself, not only novelty or escalating rewards, and newcomers can get
  instruction in the moves.
- **Question seeds:**
  - After the novelty wears off, is there a practice worth coming back to?
  - Does it reward practice itself, or only visible spurts of progress?
  - If its external rewards stopped, would the practice still hold players?
  - How does a newcomer get instruction in the moves?
- **Misapplied when:**
  - used on one-shot play. Apply it when the claim involves skill or repeat use.
  - a player type is treated as a fixed trait. The book says the types mix across
    fields and over time (p. 24).
- **Default binding:** advisory

## Calibration plant

Replace a skill progression with a new unlock every minute and no core move to practice.
The critic should cite **The Dabbler** or **The mastery curve**.

## Loop check

- **Timing:** from wave 2, and at plateau
- **Question:** Is the loop dabbling (a new approach every round), obsessing (bigger,
  riskier changes for small gains), or hacking (settling for ties and rephrased gaps)?
- **On FAIL:** adjust the process: name one diagnosis and one adjustment, and apply the
  adjustment in the next wave. At a plateau exit, include the diagnosis in the closing
  entry.
