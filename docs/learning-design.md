# Course I · learning through repeated encounters

## Owner-confirmed direction

Audience: an adult learning independently around work and everyday life.
Course rhythm: **ten canonical films over twenty weeks**, one two-week cycle per
film, with optional exploratory films for off weeks. This replaces the earlier
six-month horizon. Commute listening prepares encounters with the films; short
seated workbook sessions fit breaks and evenings. A typical workbook visit is
**5–15 minutes** (owner direction, 2026-11-17). Longer activities should unfold
over multiple visits rather than require a longer sitting. This is a design
target, not a timed task or a limit on chosen exploration.
Before/Inside/Beside/After/Bridge
roles still govern individual selections: not all listening must precede the film.

The experience is highly self-directed. Use exposure, recall, scaffolding and
successive layers of meaning. Waypoints support remembering and noticing change;
they are **not tests or rubrics**. No grades, scored checkpoints, streaks, mastery
percentages, timed recall or required demonstrations unlock the next chapter.

The four intended outcomes, recorded from the owner's answers:

| ID | Learner outcome | Design responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| O1 | Understand how filmmakers shape our understanding of music in culture | Return to specific scenes; distinguish dramatic framing, musical choices and historical evidence |
| O2 | Distinguish musical elements and relevant historical trends, including echoes in pop culture | Repeated listening and concrete comparisons; distinguish documented influence from resemblance |
| O3 | Name significant composers, artists, performers and directors and connect them to canonical works | Re-encounter people in meaningful relationships with works and moments; distinguish their roles |
| O4 | Anticipate the structure of the music covered | Hear repetition, contrast, return and larger sections; predict, listen and revise without requiring notation knowledge |

These are intended outcomes, not demonstrated effects of this untested course.
Detailed rhythm, examples and wording below are design proposals, not approved
revisions of the Amadeus manuscript or puzzle.

| Principle | Evidence-informed use | Boundary for this course |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval practice | Invite recall before re-reading or replaying; then offer a way to check or elaborate [1, 2] | Recollection can be private and unscored; do not turn every encounter into a quiz |
| Spacing | Revisit an earlier sound, person or idea after intervening time/material [1, 3] | No universal optimal interval is established here; the twenty-week schedule is an owner choice |

Research on memory, tutoring and instructional design does not directly establish
the effectiveness of a twenty-week adult film/music workbook. The pilot should
test whether these adaptations are inviting, understandable and useful. Difficulty
is not itself evidence of learning; reduce frustration and unnecessary load.

## The chapter rhythm: encounter → recall → add meaning → return

Each two-week cycle offers a route, not a deadline or a completion checklist.

1. **Encounter.** Give a short listening invitation in ordinary language: a sound,
   change or return to notice. Supply enough context to orient without explaining
   away the experience. Commute participation needs no screen, writing or score;
   anything requiring those belongs in a later seated session.
2. **Recall.** Before supplying a reminder, invite a brief memory of the sound,
   person, scene or idea. Speaking, humming, drawing, jotting or simply thinking
   are options. Let learners consult a hint or replay immediately if useful.
3. **Add a layer.** Watch the core film and examine one concrete connection:
   audible feature → filmmaker's choice → cultural/historical meaning. Introduce
   terminology after a useful encounter, alongside a clear example.
4. **Return and vary.** In the off week, replay, compare, reflect or explore an
   optional film. In a later chapter, return to one earlier idea with a genuinely
   different example. Optional films add depth and never become hidden prerequisites.

An off week can remain quiet or absorb life interruptions. A missed cycle does
not require restarting the course. The canonical film order and extension queue
remain in the course manifests; this guide does not reorder or add selections.

## Scaffolding without compulsory progression

For a listening or interpretation move, provide three available levels:

- **Listen with a guide:** model one observation and point to an audible cue.
- **Try a small nudge:** ask a focused question with an optional hint.
- **Make your own connection:** invite an observation, prediction or comparison.

These are choices, not levels to pass. Keep the guide reachable later; do not
withdraw support just because chapter numbers advance. More experienced learners
can skip explanations. Explanatory notes should identify factual errors clearly
without grading a personal interpretation or pretending all interpretations are
equally well supported. Never withhold useful help to enforce a recall attempt.

## Waypoints: private invitations, not assessment stations

Keep each visit worthwhile on its own. For a longer activity, propose natural
pause points and a brief “next time” cue so the learner can stop without losing
their place. Allow a small trace—a phrase, mark or bookmarked passage—to support
returning; do not require the learner to restart. Multiple visits should add
meaning rather than repeat setup. These are authoring implications of the owner's
time preference; exact activity segmentation still needs pilot review.

Proposed placement: a short entry bookmark, a small chapter-end recall invitation,
an optional midpoint return near week 10, and a closing return near week 20.
Longer cross-chapter connections should replace other work rather than accumulate.
Exact frequency remains a pilot question. The typical 5–15-minute visit is the
owner's design preference, not a research-prescribed optimum or a completion timer.

Example invitations, all provisional:

- **Bring something back:** “Before turning back, what sound or scene stays with
  you? If nothing comes, replay a little and begin there.”
- **Connect a person:** “Who helped make that moment—composer, performer or
  filmmaker? Make one connection, then look back at the credits or notes.”
- **Hear a shape:** “What do you expect to return? Listen on. What surprised you?”
- **Add a cultural layer:** “What did the film suggest this music was for? Does
  another scene or source complicate that idea?”
- **Return later:** “Bring back a moment from an earlier chapter. What can you
  hear or explain now that you did not notice then?”

The learner may keep a personal trace or none. There is no submission, correctness
score, rating scale or completeness requirement. Offer a source-linked listening
or context note after the invitation so mistaken factual associations need not
become rehearsed misinformation. Owners/editors still use internal acceptance
criteria for accuracy, accessibility and production; those are not learner rubrics.

## Layers of meaning across the course

Use a few recurring threads rather than fifty unrelated tasks. Candidate threads:
who controls music, how films construct an artist, and how musical return/contrast
shapes expectation. Connect people and works to those threads.

An observation can grow from “something returned,” to an audible pattern, to a
filmmaker's use of that pattern, to an institution/cultural setting, and finally
to a comparison with another work. Different works require different musical
models; do not force every selection into the same form or assume an unfamiliar
piece will behave predictably. Choose a pop-culture comparison with a source or
label it explicitly as an interpretive resemblance.

## Applying this to the Amadeus pilot

- **Who’s Who:** names should connect people, roles and film moments. Preserve
  the exact existing puzzle/key; propose support outside the puzzle only after
  inspecting the approved assets. Shorter instructions must preserve the action.
- **Mozart’s Hand:** model one inspectable feature of a genuine facsimile, then
  invite the learner's own noticing. A selected, legible, rights-checked source
  matters more than additional exposition or decorative archive imagery.
- **The Commendatore Scene:** retain the exact scene assignment and a sparse
  invitation. Listening carries the demand; no detailed listening worksheet or
  extra recall task is required during the scene. A later return can connect it
  to film framing and myth-making. This applies the Inverse Play Law.
