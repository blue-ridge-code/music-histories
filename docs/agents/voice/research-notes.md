## Findings per component

### A1 — Tone

- The attitude expressed in the writing toward subject and reader; must suit audience
  and purpose (Wheaton, NN/g).
- NN/g's four dimensions, each a spectrum: **funny ↔ serious, formal ↔ casual,
  respectful ↔ irreverent, enthusiastic ↔ matter-of-fact**. A tone profile is a point
  in this 4-D space; users reliably notice dimension shifts.
- Wheaton's spectra: objective ↔ subjective, logical ↔ emotional, intimate ↔ distant,
  serious ↔ humorous.
- Small wording shifts move a dimension ("We apologize for the error" → "Oops!").
- NN/g: consider the reader's emotional state on arrival; distill tone into 3–5 target
  tone words plus anti-tone words; validate with user testing.

### A2 — Diction

- Word choice (Wheaton). Formality levels: formal / casual / slang ("are not angry" /
  "aren't mad" / "ain't ticked").
- Denotation vs connotation; positive vs negative charge ("stance" vs "spin");
  verb precision ("said" vs "declared" vs "insisted", Journalism U).
- Jargon calibrated to audience expertise; avoid clichés, vagueness, wordiness;
  "omit needless words" (Strunk & White via Journalism U).
- Concrete vs abstract diction: **[craft-standard]**.

### A3 — Syntax

- Arrangement of words into sentences: length, complexity, order (Journalism U).
- Short/punchy = urgency and clarity; long/flowing = atmosphere or complex argument;
  good writers vary length deliberately (Journalism U, Wheaton).
- Word order for emphasis; active over passive in direct prose (Journalism U).
- Simple/compound/complex types, inversion, parallelism: **[craft-standard]**.

### A4 — Pacing

- Speed of the story or argument for the reader. Sources cover it thinly: short
  sentences accelerate (Journalism U); features tolerate "a slower, more deliberate
  pace" than news.
- Action vs description vs exposition ratio, scene vs summary, detail density:
  **[craft-standard]** — the classic pacing levers, ungrounded in the fetched pages.

### A5 — Point of view

- First ("I") = intimacy, direct access to a mind; second ("you") = reader on the
  field, rare; third = most common, most freedom (LitCharts).
- Third-person omniscient vs limited; narrative distance (first person removes the
  intermediary); narrator need not be the protagonist; alternating POV as a
  deliberate device (LitCharts).

### B1 — Rhythm

- The beat made by varied sentence length; elegant style has "a noticeable flow or
  rhythm" (Journalism U). Pauses as expressive material — "the pauses between the
  notes — ah, that is where the art resides" (Schnabel, via Wheaton).
- Stress patterns, euphony: **[craft-standard]**.
- Rhythm vs pacing: **[craft-standard]** — rhythm is sentence-level texture; pacing is
  the large-scale speed of the narrative or argument. This split is the framework's.

### B2 — Persona

- The constructed speaker — "the voice or character adopted by the author," not
  necessarily the author; distinct from narrator (Literary Devices).
- Define the persona's age, background, beliefs, motivations before writing; keep the
  mask consistent — "inconsistencies can undermine the reader's trust"; reliable vs
  intentionally unreliable (Literary Devices).
- Relation to voice: voice is the persistent fingerprint across a body of work
  (Wheaton, Journalism U); persona is the character carrying it in one piece. POV is
  the technical frame; persona is who stands inside it (Literary Devices).

### B3 — Attitude

- The sources largely conflate attitude with tone (Wheaton: "tone, or attitude";
  NN/g: "feelings towards the subject"). This framework's split — A1 tone = attitude
  *aimed* at topic/reader, B3 attitude = the *ambient* emotional undercurrent — is a
  deliberate refinement, **not** sourced.
- Carriers: Wheaton's logical ↔ emotional and intimate ↔ distant spectra; NN/g's
  enthusiastic ↔ matter-of-fact energy; connotation-loaded diction (A2).

## Developing voice and keeping it consistent

- Maturation path (Journalism U): imitate admired writers → practice and experiment
  across audiences and topics → authenticity as imitation fades. Regimen: read widely
  and critically, write daily, omit needless words, adapt the same event into
  multiple styles as training.
- Consistency is layered (Wheaton): switching styles mid-piece distracts and hurts
  believability, but tone *should* flex with the situation while voice — "the
  essential, individual thoughts and expression" — stays constant.
- NN/g operationalizes this for organizations: fix a profile on the four dimensions,
  distill to tone words + anti-tone words, validate with user testing.
- All three sources agree style is chosen per audience and purpose: inform →
  plain/concise, persuade → forcible, engage → elegant (Journalism U); mismatched
  complexity frustrates lay readers, oversimplification costs credibility with
  experts.
