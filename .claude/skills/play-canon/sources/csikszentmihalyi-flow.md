## Core concepts

- **Eight elements.** The book reports eight components of enjoyment, of which people
  mention at least one and often all (p. 49). The next eight concepts are those
  components, in the book's order.
- **Completable task.** The experience usually comes when we face a task we have a
  chance of completing (p. 49).
- **Concentration.** We must be able to concentrate on what we are doing (p. 49).
- **Clear goals.** The task has clear goals, so the person knows what to do next
  (p. 49).
- **Immediate feedback.** The task gives immediate feedback on how well we're doing
  (p. 49).
- **Effortless involvement.** A deep but effortless involvement pushes the worries and
  frustrations of everyday life out of awareness (p. 49).
- **Sense of control.** Enjoyable experiences let people exercise a sense of control
  over their actions (p. 49).
- **Loss of self-consciousness.** Concern for the self disappears during the activity,
  yet the sense of self comes out stronger once it is over (p. 49).
- **Altered time.** Hours pass like minutes, and minutes can stretch out like hours
  (p. 49).
- **Challenge and skill.** Enjoyment appears at the boundary between boredom and
  anxiety, where challenges just balance the person's capacity to act. A mismatched
  tennis game leaves the weaker player anxious and the stronger one bored (p. 52). A
  bored player gets back to flow by taking on a harder challenge, an anxious one by
  building skill, and each return lands at a higher level of complexity. That is why
  flow activities lead to growth: "One cannot enjoy doing the same thing at the same
  level for long" (pp. 74-75).
- **Autotelic.** From the Greek *auto* (self) and *telos* (goal): an activity done not
  for some future benefit but because the doing itself is the reward (p. 67).
- **Flow activities.** Some activities are designed to make flow easier. They have rules
  that require learning skills, they set goals, give feedback, and make control
  possible, and they set themselves apart from everyday reality with uniforms and
  special places (p. 72). Competition helps only while attention stays on the activity;
  when the point becomes beating the opponent, impressing an audience, or a contract, it
  distracts instead (p. 73).
- **Perceived challenge.** What counts is not the real challenges and skills but the ones
  the person is aware of. One person answers the challenge of a mountain peak and ignores
  a piece of music; another does the opposite (p. 75).
- **Psychic entropy.** Disorder in consciousness: information that conflicts with
  existing intentions or distracts us from carrying them out, felt as pain, fear, rage,
  anxiety, or jealousy. It pulls attention away from what we chose (p. 36).
- **Complexity.** After flow, the self is more complex. Complexity combines
  differentiation (moving toward uniqueness) and integration (union with other people
  and with ideas beyond the self) (p. 41).

## Guidance

### Interactions

- Make the next action obvious, and show its result within seconds. (Clear goals,
  Immediate feedback)
- Give each stretch of play a task the player has a real chance of completing.
  (Completable task)
- Let players choose or adjust difficulty, and raise the challenge as skill grows.
  (Challenge and skill)
- Give actions predictable effects, so failures are the player's to learn from. (Sense
  of control)
- In competitive play, keep attention on the activity: the rival's skill is the
  challenge, not beating them the prize. (Flow activities)

### Structures

- Build a difficulty curve that climbs in steps, with short recovery stretches between
  them. (Challenge and skill)
- Nest goals: small goals inside larger ones, each with its own feedback. (Clear goals)
- Make the next challenge visible, since players respond to the challenges they can see.
  (Perceived challenge)
- Size sessions to the core activity, with no forced breaks mid-flow. (Concentration)

### Content

- Write feedback that informs (what happened, and why) rather than feedback that only
  rewards. (Immediate feedback)

### Style

- Cut noise. Interface, text, and sound should support concentration, not compete with
  it. (Psychic entropy)
- Let the activity be its own reward; keep extrinsic reward clutter to a minimum.
  (Autotelic)

## Anti-patterns

- **Interruptions in the core activity.** Pop-ups, notifications, and forced cutscenes
  mid-flow. (Psychic entropy)
- **Fixed difficulty.** Challenge that tips into anxiety or boredom and stays there.
  (Challenge and skill)
- **Vague goals and slow feedback.** The player can't tell what to do or whether it
  worked. (Clear goals, Immediate feedback)
- **Reward clutter.** Extrinsic rewards crowding out the activity itself. (Autotelic)
- **Prize-focused competition.** Competition whose attention goes to beating others,
  the audience, or the prize rather than the activity. (Flow activities)

## Review criterion

- **Set:** Csikszentmihalyi
- **Source:** *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* (1990), Csikszentmihalyi
- **Concepts:** Eight elements, Completable task, Concentration, Clear goals, Immediate
  feedback, Effortless involvement, Sense of control, Loss of self-consciousness, Altered
  time, Challenge and skill, Flow activities, Perceived challenge, Autotelic, Psychic
  entropy, Complexity
- **Criterion:** Goals are clear, feedback comes within seconds, challenge rises with
  skill, and nothing breaks concentration during the core activity.
- **Question seeds:**
  - At [moment], can the player tell within seconds what to do next and whether it
    worked?
  - Does challenge rise with skill, or is there a step that tips into anxiety or boredom?
  - What interrupts concentration during the core activity?
- **Misapplied when:**
  - every piece is expected to produce flow. For relaxed or social play, judge only
    whether goals and feedback are clear.
  - flow is scored from the design alone. The book warns that the challenges and skills
    a player perceives are what count (p. 75).
- **Default binding:** yes

## Calibration plant

Add a five-second unskippable reward animation after every move in a fast-paced puzzle.
The critic should cite **Psychic entropy** or **Immediate feedback**.

## Fit

- **Strong for:** skill play, interfaces, learning sequences, and writing meant to be
  read in one sitting (goal clarity and momentum).
- **Weak for:** social or ceremonial play whose value isn't absorption.
