## Core concepts

- **Metacommunication.** At the Fleishhacker Zoo in 1952, Bateson watched two young
  monkeys whose actions resembled combat but were plainly "not combat". Play can happen
  only if players can metacommunicate: exchange signals that carry the message "This is
  play" (p. 316).
- **The nip.** "The playful nip denotes the bite, but it does not denote what would be
  denoted by the bite" (p. 317). A play action stands for another action without its
  consequences.
- **Paradox.** Spelled out, "This is play" says: these actions do not denote what the
  actions they stand for would denote. It is a paradox of the Epimenides type (p. 317).
  In play, map and territory are both equated and told apart (p. 321).
- **Map and territory.** A message is not the thing it denotes; the word "cat" can't
  scratch us. Play may have been an important step in the evolution of communication
  (p. 317).
- **The play-threat complex.** Threat is like play: the clenched fist stands for a punch
  that hasn't happened (p. 317). Histrionic play, bluff, playful threat, and teasing form
  one complex. Gambling and playing with risk grow from the combination of threat and
  play, and spectatorship belongs to the same field (p. 318).
- **Frame trouble.** The frame "This is play" is labile. The line between map and
  territory can always break down: in Andaman Islands peace-making, ritual blows can be
  taken for real ones, and the ceremony becomes a battle (p. 318). A mismatch hurts too:
  an exchange is a game for one person and serious for the other, and it goes well only
  when both hold the same idea of it (*Steps*, p. 27).
- **Framed intensity.** Play and therapy both happen inside a bounded psychological
  frame, and their messages stand in a special relation to reality: the pretend combat
  of play is not real combat. The frame is what lets the pretend reach its full
  intensity and still be talked about (*Steps*, p. 197).
- **Evolving rules.** A rigid game like canasta changes only if the players stop and
  discuss its rules. Play like therapy, or kittens' play, is an evolving system: its
  rules are implicit, and changes are proposed by moves made inside the play itself
  (*Steps*, pp. 197-198). The metalogue calls such play "a game whose purpose is to
  discover the rules, which rules are always changing" (*Steps*, p. 30). Without these
  paradoxes, communication would be "a game with rigid rules, unrelieved by change or
  humor" (*Steps*, p. 198).
- **Is-this-play games.** A more complex form of play is built not on the premise "This is
  play" but around the question "Is this play?". Initiation hazing is one of its ritual
  forms (p. 318).
- **Reminders.** Within play, a player often has to be reminded that "This is play"
  (p. 321).
- **Frames.** Frames are psychological, and are often named in everyday words: "play",
  "movie", "interview", "job" (p. 322). A frame is exclusive and inclusive at once. Like
  a picture frame it says "Attend to what is within and do not attend to what is
  outside", and it tells the viewer to read what's inside differently from the wallpaper
  beyond it. Every metacommunicative message sets a frame, down to punctuation marks
  (p. 323).

## Guidance

### Interactions

- Send a clear "this is play" signal at first contact, through tone, visual language,
  or a playful opening move. (Metacommunication)
- Use play signals that stand for real acts without their consequences: mock threats,
  pretend stakes, a teasing bump. (The nip)
- Remind players they're in play at the moments they might forget, not only at the
  start. (Reminders)
- When a game deliberately asks "Is this play?", make the stakes recoverable and the
  exit obvious. (Is-this-play games)
- Let players change the rules through play itself: a move that proposes a new rule,
  taken up by the others' responses, without stopping the game. (Evolving rules)

### Structures

- Make the frame's edges visible: "attend to what's inside, not outside". Menus,
  settings, and exits read as outside the play; moves and characters read as inside it.
  (Frames)
- Re-signal the frame at every change of mode, such as setup, play, payment, error, or
  ending. (Frames)
- When the work plays with the frame itself (a fourth-wall break, a prank, a twist),
  signal it and let the player recover. (Paradox)

### Content

- Mark pretend content as pretend: mock danger, fake money, and invented authority that
  look unmistakably staged. (The nip)
- Treat bluff, teasing, dares, and chance as one family, and handle risk play with the
  care that family needs. (The play-threat complex)
- Use a clear frame to let players take strong material (fear, loss, rivalry, grief) at
  full intensity, with a way to talk about it afterward. (Framed intensity)

### Style

- Keep tone markers consistent (typography, sound, voice), so players always know which
  frame they're in. Small signals frame too, down to punctuation. (Metacommunication,
  Frames)

## Anti-patterns

- **Real demands in play clothing.** Payments, data requests, or commitments styled as
  game moves. (Frame trouble)
- **Signals that can be read literally.** A mock threat that reads as a real one; a
  teasing message that reads as an insult. (The nip, Frame trouble)
- **Frame drift.** The tone changes and nothing signals the change. (Frames)
- **Mismatched frames.** One player treats an exchange as a game while another takes
  it seriously, and nothing brings them to the same frame. (Frame trouble)
- **Hazing by design.** Ambiguity about whether it's play, used to test, pressure, or
  humiliate newcomers. (Is-this-play games)

## Review criterion

- **Set:** Bateson
- **Source:** "A Theory of Play and Fantasy" (1955), Bateson
- **Concepts:** Metacommunication, The nip, Paradox, Map and territory, The play-threat
  complex, Frame trouble, Framed intensity, Evolving rules, Is-this-play games,
  Reminders, Frames
- **Criterion:** "This is play" is signaled clearly at first contact, renewed when
  players might forget, and kept consistent through every change of mode, so play is
  never read as real and no real demand hides behind play.
- **Question seeds:**
  - At first contact, what says "this is play", and would a newcomer read it that way?
  - Is there a moment where a playful signal could be taken literally, or where a real
    demand hides behind play?
  - When the piece changes mode (setup, error, payment, ending), do the frame signals
    stay consistent?
  - Where the piece plays with "Is this play?", can a player who misreads it recover?
- **Misapplied when:** deliberate play with the frame is failed. A signaled, recoverable
  twist is play about play.
- **Default binding:** yes

## Calibration plant

An in-game "surprise" message styled exactly like a real account-security warning. The
critic should cite **Frame trouble**.

## Fit

- **Strong for:** humor, pretend play, social play, games with purchases or data
  collection, and writing that shifts tone.
- **Weak for:** solitary abstract puzzles with a single, stable mode.
- **Overlaps:**
  - Carse (Changing rules) on rules that change during play;
  - Sutton-Smith (Variability) on staging real risks where they can be survived.
