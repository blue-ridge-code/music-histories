## Core concepts

- **Finite and infinite games.** "A finite game is played for the purpose of winning, an
  infinite game for the purpose of continuing the play" (p. 3). A finite game must come
  to a definitive end when someone wins (p. 3). The only purpose of an infinite game is
  to keep it from ending, to keep everyone in play (p. 7).
- **Boundaries.** A finite game has agreed temporal, spatial, and numerical boundaries:
  a beginning and an end, a marked area, and selected players (p. 4). An infinite game
  has none, and anyone who wishes may play (p. 7). Infinite players take limits into
  play: "Finite players play within boundaries; infinite players play with boundaries"
  (p. 10).
- **Changing rules.** The rules of a finite game can't change during play. The rules of
  an infinite game must change, whenever the play is threatened by a finite outcome. They
  are like the grammar of a living language (p. 9).
- **Bringing others in.** Infinite players change the rules to keep anyone from winning
  and to bring as many people as possible into the play (p. 9).
- **Nesting.** "Finite games can be played within an infinite game, but an infinite game
  cannot be played within a finite game" (p. 7). Infinite players enter finite games
  with full energy, but take them up playfully rather than seriously (p. 14). Evil is not
  the inclusion of finite games in an infinite game but the restriction of all play to
  one finite game (p. 33).
- **Theatrical and dramatic.** Finite play is theatrical: it is meant to conclude, and its
  roles are scripted and performed for an audience. Infinite play is dramatic: it keeps
  the future open and makes every script useless (p. 16). A finite game is dramatic while
  it lasts, but only provisionally. Once it ends, every move looks as if it led
  inevitably to the winner (p. 17).
- **Training and education.** "To be prepared against surprise is to be trained. To be
  prepared for surprise is to be educated" (p. 19). Surprise decides most finite games
  (p. 17); the infinite player expects to be transformed by it (pp. 18-19). Education
  finds the past unfinished, and training treats it as finished (p. 19).
- **Serious and playful.** Seriousness belongs to roles and an established script. We are
  playful when we meet others at the level of choice, with no outcome imposed beyond the
  decision to continue. Playful doesn't mean trivial or frivolous (p. 15).
- **Titles.** What one wins in a finite game is a title. Titles are public, and work only
  if others see them (p. 19). A title points backward to a finished game and
  "effectively takes a person out of play" (pp. 26-27).
- **Power and strength.** Power belongs only to finite games; the infinite player plays
  with strength instead. Power is freedom within limits, and strength is freedom with
  limits. Power is limited to a selected few, but "anyone can be strong". Infinite players
  act so that others respond by starting actions of their own (p. 31).
- **Obligation.** It is a principle of all play, finite and infinite, that whoever plays,
  plays freely: "Whoever must play, cannot play" (p. 4).
- **Horizon.** A boundary is where opposing forces meet. A horizon is never reached;
  moving toward it only brings a new horizon (p. 57). Each play of an infinite game opens
  a new horizon of time (p. 7).

## Guidance

### Interactions

- Let players change a rule, a goal, or a use as part of play, especially when the play
  would otherwise end in a win. (Changing rules)
- Offer ways to keep playing after the ending: new modes, remixes, handing the game on to
  others. (Finite and infinite games)
- Favor moves that bring people in over moves that knock them out, where the claim is
  open play. (Bringing others in)
- Design moves that invite other players to answer with moves of their own, rather than
  moves that settle the matter. (Power and strength)

### Structures

- Nest finite games (a match, a level, a course) inside an open frame, so finishing one
  opens the next instead of closing play. (Nesting)
- Keep outcomes open (dramatic) where the claim is open play; use scripted arcs
  (theatrical) only where intended. (Theatrical and dramatic)
- Make each milestone reveal more to explore: a horizon rather than a wall. (Horizon)

### Content

- Choose content that invites reinterpretation and rewards being ready for surprise.
  (Training and education)

### Style

- Keep the register playful rather than serious: meet players at the level of choice,
  without making the play trivial. (Serious and playful)

## Anti-patterns

- **Win and done.** The ending closes play and nothing invites a return, in work whose
  claim is open play. (Finite and infinite games)
- **One game for everything.** All play reduced to a single finite game, such as one
  leaderboard that ranks everything a player does. (Nesting)
- **Titles as the point.** Badges and ranks that freeze players into past wins and take
  them out of play. (Titles)
- **Scripted surprise.** "Surprises" that are fully determined, which train the player
  rather than educate. (Training and education)
- **Obligatory play.** Required participation labeled as play. (Obligation)

## Review criterion

- **Set:** Carse
- **Source:** *Finite and Infinite Games: A Vision of Life as Play and Possibility*
  (1986), Carse
- **Concepts:** Finite and infinite games, Boundaries, Changing rules, Bringing others in,
  Nesting, Theatrical and dramatic, Training and education, Serious and playful, Titles,
  Power and strength, Obligation, Horizon
- **Criterion:** Where the claim wants open play, the work invites continuing, remixing,
  or coming back, and players can reshape its boundaries rather than only play inside
  them.
- **Question seeds:**
  - After the end condition, does the artifact invite continuing, remixing, or coming
    back?
  - Can players reshape a boundary (a rule, a use, a goal), or only play inside it?
  - Do its finite games (levels, matches, courses) sit inside something that continues,
    or does one of them end everything?
  - Is it theatrical where the claim wants it dramatic?
- **Misapplied when:** finite games are failed for being finite. The book holds that
  finite games can be played within an infinite game (p. 7); the failure is restricting
  all play to one finite game (p. 33). A race is allowed a winner when the claim wants a
  race.
- **Default binding:** advisory

## Calibration plant

Where the claim says "something you come back to", add a hard ending that locks the
toy or game with no replay, remix, or continuation. The critic should cite **Finite and
infinite games** or **Nesting**.

## Loop check

- **Timing:** at exit (Win or Budget)
- **Question:** Is the win ending a game worth continuing? Would raising or reframing the
  bar give more play than stopping?
- **On FAIL:** propose a raised or reframed bar to the owner. Only the owner raises the
  bar.

## Fit

- **Strong for:** open-ended play, communities, toys and tools meant for repeat use, and
  the loop's own exit decision.
- **Weak for:** deliberately finite experiences such as a single puzzle, a race, or an
  escape room.
- **Tension:** Sutton-Smith counts voluntariness as a modern Western value rather than
  part of what play is (see the Sutton-Smith source). When both sets are chosen, send
  findings that rest only on **Obligation** to the owner as a tension.
- **Overlaps:**
  - Illich (Measurement) and Leonard (prizes and medals) also flag rewards that stand in
    for play, as **Titles** does;
  - Bateson (Evolving rules) also covers rules that change during play, as **Changing
    rules** does.

  When two chosen sets would flag the same gap, keep one per run.
