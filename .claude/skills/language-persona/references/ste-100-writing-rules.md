# STE-100 writing rules — distilled instruction set

Derived from **ASD-STE100 Issue 9 (2025-01-15)** (private repo; licensed standard — the PDF
`ASD-STE100_ISSUE9.pdf` in this folder is the standard of record). All 53 rules of Part 1,
organized by the standard's 9 sections. Rule statements are faithful condensations of the
official rule texts; examples are adapted from the standard.

**How agents use this file:** at draft or review time, read the section(s) matching the
writing task — procedures → Sections 5 and 7; descriptions → Section 6; sentences/verbs →
Sections 3–4; word choice → Sections 1–2 (+ grep `ste-100-dictionary.md`). Do not read this
file end-to-end into context. If a rule looks wrong, verify against the PDF.

---

## Section 1 — Words (rules 1.1–1.14)

Which words you may use, and how.

- **1.1** Use only words that are: approved in the dictionary, technical nouns, or
  technical verbs. (A technical noun/verb is a term for a specific concept or process in
  your subject field — in this template, those live in the project's `CONTEXT.md`.)
- **1.2** Use an approved word only as its specified part of speech. "Test" is an approved
  noun, not a verb: write "Do the leak test," not "Test the system for leaks."
- **1.3** Use an approved word only with its approved meaning. "Follow" = "come after" —
  "Do the procedures that follow" is STE; "Follow the safety instructions" is not.
- **1.4** Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives (the dictionary lists them).
- **1.5–1.6** You may use a word that is not dictionary-approved only when it is a
  technical noun, or part of one, in an accepted category.
- **1.7** Never use a technical noun as a verb.
- **1.8–1.9** Use technical nouns approved for your company/industry/field; when choosing,
  prefer the short, easy-to-understand one.
- **1.10** No regional, slang, or jargon words as technical nouns.
- **1.11** One item, one name — never use different technical nouns for the same thing.
- **1.12–1.13** Technical verbs are permitted within accepted categories; never use a
  technical verb as a noun.
- **1.14** American English spelling, unless an official directive says otherwise.

Operational guidance from the section body:

- Replacing a non-approved word must not change the sentence's meaning; if the approved
  alternative shifts meaning, change the construction instead
  ("Make sure that the valve can operate," not "is operable").
- Word not in the dictionary? Find it in an English dictionary, pick the best approved
  synonym, or rebuild the sentence with approved words.

## Section 2 — Multi-word nouns (rules 2.1–2.2)

- **2.1** Keep multi-word nouns to a maximum of **three words**. Break longer clusters
  with prepositions: not "runway light connection resistance calibration" (5 words) but
  "calibration of the resistance of the runway light connection."
- **2.2** When a technical noun must exceed three words: write it in full on first use,
  then either give a shorter approved form or hyphenate the words that act as one unit.

## Section 3 — Verbs (rules 3.1–3.7)

- **3.1** Use only the verb forms the dictionary lists for each approved verb.
- **3.2** Permitted tenses/forms only: infinitive, imperative (commands), simple present,
  simple past, simple future, past participle as an adjective. No continuous/perfect forms.
- **3.3** Use the past participle only as an adjective.
- **3.4** Do not stack auxiliary verbs into complex constructions.
- **3.5** Use the "-ing" form only as a technical noun or as a modifier inside one.
- **3.6** Active voice. In descriptive writing, passive is allowed only when the agent is
  unknown. To activate agent-less passives, make the reader "you" or your organization
  "we": "The volume control can be adjusted" → "Adjust the volume control" (procedure) or
  "You can adjust the volume control" (description).
- **3.7** Describe actions with approved verbs, not nouns: "The ohmmeter shows 450 ohms,"
  not "gives an indication of"; "Before you remove the unit," not "Before the removal of
  the unit." If the word is not approved as a verb, rebuild: "Do a check of the battery,"
  not "Check the battery."

## Section 4 — Sentences (rules 4.1–4.5)

- **4.1** Short, clear sentences. Procedures: direct imperative instructions — one long
  tangled step becomes a numbered sequence ("1. Remove the cover assembly as follows:
  A. Remove the four screws… B. Remove the cover…"). Descriptions: one topic per
  sentence, no imperatives; develop the topic across following sentences.
- **4.2** Do not omit words or use contractions to shorten sentences.
- **4.3** Use vertical lists for complex text.
- **4.4** Use connecting words/phrases to link sentences on related topics.
- **4.5** Put an article (the, a, an) or demonstrative adjective (this, these) before a
  noun or multi-word noun where applicable.

## Section 5 — Procedural writing (rules 5.1–5.5)

- **5.1** Maximum **20 words** per sentence in procedures — including warnings, cautions,
  and safety instructions. Split longer ones where actions allow
  (25 words → "Put preservation oil into the unit through the vent hole. (10) Continue
  until the oil level is… (16)").
- **5.2** One instruction per sentence, unless the actions happen at the same time.
- **5.3** Write instructions in the imperative (command) form.
- **5.4** If a condition matters first, open with a descriptive statement, then a comma,
  then the command.
- **5.5** Notes give information only — never instructions.

## Section 6 — Descriptive writing (rules 6.1–6.6)

- **6.1** Give information gradually — one subject per sentence; never the imperative form
  in descriptive text.
- **6.2** Repeat key words and key phrases to give the text a logical structure; once
  chosen, never vary them. Approved connectors include "and," "but," "then," "thus,"
  "as a result," "at the same time."
- **6.3** Maximum **25 words** per descriptive sentence.
- **6.4–6.6** Paragraphs group related information, one topic per paragraph, and never
  more than **six sentences** per paragraph.

## Section 7 — Safety instructions (rules 7.1–7.3)

Definitions: a **warning** = risk of injury or death; a **caution** = risk of damage to
objects. (Other industries may use "danger"/"notice" — the content rules still apply.)

- **7.1** Mark the risk level with the applicable word (or symbol). Do a real risk
  analysis: injury/death risk → WARNING; equipment-damage risk → CAUTION; both → WARNING.
- **7.2** Start with a clear, accurate command or condition.
- **7.3** Then explain the risk or possible result. Never write an abstract slogan —
  not "CAUTION: EXTREME CLEANLINESS OF OXYGEN TUBES IS IMPERATIVE" but "WARNING: MAKE
  SURE THAT THE OXYGEN TUBES ARE FULLY CLEAN. OXYGEN AND GREASE MAKE AN EXPLOSIVE
  MIXTURE. AN EXPLOSION CAN CAUSE INJURY OR DEATH."

## Section 8 — Punctuation and word count (rules 8.1–8.7)

- **8.1** All standard punctuation is allowed **except the semicolon (;)** — it invites
  long sentences. Write two sentences instead.
- **8.2** Hyphenate directly related words: adjective clusters before a noun
  ("low-altitude flight"), fractions ("forty-seven"), shape terms ("L-shaped bracket"),
  noun-first verbs ("heat-treat"), vowel-meeting prefixes ("de-icing").
- **8.3** Parentheses only for: illustration/text references, item identifiers, work-step
  letters, abbreviations, singular+plural "(s)", explanations, alternatives.
- **8.4** In a vertical list, a colon ends the sentence for word-count purposes, like a
  period.
- **8.5** A parenthetical counts as one word.
- **8.6** Each of these counts as one word: numbers, numbers with units, abbreviations,
  alphanumeric identifiers, quoted text, titles/headings/labels, proper nouns.
- **8.7** A hyphenated term counts as one word.

## Section 9 — Writing practices (rules 9.1–9.4 + general recommendations)

- **9.1** When a word-for-word swap is not enough, rebuild the sentence — preserving
  meaning: "Cycle the unit twice" → "Operate the unit for two cycles."
- **9.2** Use each approved word correctly: one approved meaning, one approved part of
  speech. "Wear" is approved only as "become damaged by friction" — so "Use (or put on)
  protective clothing," never "wear." "Above/below" are physical positions only, never
  limits ("become less than 20 psi"). "See" is eyes only, never "come to know."
- **9.3** Do not create phrasal verbs ("goes down" for pressure → "decreases").
- **9.4** Once terminology or wording is selected, keep it consistent everywhere.

General recommendations:

- **GR-1** Keep the conjunction "that": "Make sure that the valve is open."
- **GR-2** Beware ambiguous "with" — re-read every use ("Install the panel with the green
  fasteners" has three readings); name the primary action verb ("Seal the opening with
  tool TS9867," not "Use tool TS9867 to…").
- **GR-3 / GR-4** Pronouns must have one unmistakable referent — repeat the noun instead
  when in doubt, especially "this."
- **GR-5** False friends: check words against their English meaning, not a look-alike in
  your native language.
- **GR-6** No Latin abbreviations — write "for example," "that is"; drop "etc."
- **GR-7** Gender-neutral language always ("he/she," "man/woman" not permitted unless the
  context requires it, e.g. medical texts).
- **GR-8** Possessive form is permitted but use it correctly; when unsure, rewrite
  ("the instructions of the manufacturer").
