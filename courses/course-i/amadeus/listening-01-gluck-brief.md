## What to keep from the capture

The useful contribution is a sequence of real listener questions:

1. Who is Gluck, and why is this music here before Amadeus?
2. Who made this recording: composer, singer, conductor, orchestra, choir and label?
3. What is happening dramatically, and what do the Italian titles mean?
4. How might a repeated musical idea carry changing emotional meaning?

Use these as optional layers, not a biography dossier or four required activities.
A brief identification and one useful listening invitation can come first;
performer glossary, dramatic context and verified text can be revisited in later
5–15-minute encounters. Retain the Before role and distinguish the broader opera
from the aria. A first encounter should work without reading during the commute.

Question 4 is a candidate direction for musical analysis, not a claim that the
recording has already been analyzed or that a particular form has been verified.
The connection to Amadeus needs a source-grounded explanation, not a simplistic
story in which Gluck single-handedly replaces an inferior earlier style.

## What not to import as established fact

- The capture promises side-by-side lyrics but supplies scene summaries in that
  section. A synopsis is not an exact text or translation.
- Historically informed performance should not be described as recovering the
  exact sound Gluck heard. Instrument choices and interpretation need careful,
  attributed explanation rather than that absolute claim.
- Composer nationality, court appointments, detailed training, influence on later
  composers and claims about entire operatic eras need appropriate verification
  before becoming learner copy. Do not treat search summaries as that verification.
- Claims about key, form and emotion require listening/score analysis. A major
  key alone does not settle a character's emotional state.
- Do not reuse an unattributed modern translation or make an AI-generated image
  appear to be archival evidence.
